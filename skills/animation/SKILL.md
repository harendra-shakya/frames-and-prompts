# Animation and film direction

Animation is the change of pose, shape, expression, staging, or space **over time**. A stationary image with a zoom is a motion-comic technique, not character animation. Either may be correct when chosen intentionally; do not substitute one for the other.

## Choose the form deliberately

- **Character animation:** acting, anticipation, weight, follow-through, expression changes, timed poses.
- **Motion comic:** panel crops, parallax layers, selective motion, lettering reveals, narration and sound.
- **Cinematic film:** coverage, blocking, motivated camera, performance, lighting, production sound.
- **Motion design:** graphic hierarchy, typography, timing, shape transitions, repeated visual rules.
- **Game animation:** responsive looping states, recoveries, readable hit and movement timings.

Choose the approach that fits story, time, and quality. Never describe an asset as "fully animated" if only the camera moves across a still.

## Research the movement, not just the look

- Performance/action: [Sakugabooru](https://www.sakugabooru.com/), [Sakuga Video](https://sakuga.video/), [11 Second Club](https://11secondclub.com/).
- Camera vocabulary: [Runway camera examples](https://help.runwayml.com/hc/en-us/articles/47313504791059-Camera-Terms-Prompts-Examples), [AI Camera Movements](https://godofskills.com/guides/ai-camera-movements).
- Motion graphics: [Eyecandy](https://eyecannndy.com/), [Prompt Motion](https://promptmotion.org/), [Jitter](https://jitter.video/templates/).
- Generation behavior: [Runway Gen-4 video guide](https://help.runwayml.com/hc/en-us/articles/39789879462419-Gen-4-Video-Prompting-Guide).

Record what happens in the reference **across time**: anticipation → action → reaction, motion arcs, easing, cut placement, sound cues. A screenshot alone cannot establish animation timing.

## Build the sequence

1. Write the emotional change and what the audience must understand.
2. Break it into beats, then shots. For each shot specify **starting state → physical action → ending state**, staging, lens/framing, camera motive, duration range, and transition intent.
3. Lock character/environment reference sheets, aspect ratio, frame rate, palette, texture, scale, and continuity anchors.
4. Generate or animate the highest-risk motion first (face acting, hands, contact, interaction, rapid action, transformations). Verify it before producing all shots.
5. For image-to-video, make an intentional first frame and describe **motion**, not an entirely new conflicting image. Use reference/keyframe controls if supported.
6. Assemble the edit. Add dialogue, singing, voice-over, ambience, foley, music, and captions **only where the brief calls for them**, with timing that supports the action.
7. Review the entire video with audio, then sample the first, middle, and last frames of each shot plus every transition.

## Motion and sound checks

- Does each moving object keep its identity, volume, physical contact, and consistent direction?
- Does the pose change naturally? Is there anticipation, clear action, settle or reaction where appropriate?
- Does the cut preserve geography and screen direction, or intentionally break it?
- Does camera motion serve a reveal or emotion? Is the viewer able to track the subject?
- For speech/singing: are the words intelligible, timing believable, mouth performance appropriate, and music/voice balanced?
- Are audio edits clean? Is there accidental silence, clipping, desynchronization, abrupt music reset, or unmotivated stock sound?
- Does the opening establish interest and the ending resolve the beat? Are any frames frozen or visibly morphing?

**Reject when:** the result is essentially static despite an animation brief, characters melt or drift, action is physically incoherent, dialogue/lyrics are missing, lip sync is conspicuously wrong, or visual polish masks an empty scene.

**Deliver:** playable video, shot list/timing, audio sources or notes, what you watched and the specific corrections made.
